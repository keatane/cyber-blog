**Vibe Hacking.**

It is an emerging concept in the Cyber Security landscape, reflecting a significant change in the way technical capabilities can be accessed and used.

The idea is simple: describe what you want to achieve in natural language, let an AI system help you implement it and refine the result until it works.

In software development, this approach is commonly associated with *vibe coding*. When applied to offensive security, the same principle can be used to explore vulnerabilities, develop attack scenarios, automate technical tasks and experiment with techniques that would traditionally require a more substantial technical background.

**What happens when the ability to create an attack is no longer limited to people with advanced technical skills?**

AI does not automatically make everyone a skilled attacker, nor does it guarantee that generated code or attack techniques will work. However, it can reduce the time and effort required to move from an idea to a working implementation.

And this is where things become interesting.

**Red Team.**

Red teaming has always been about thinking like an attacker to understand how an organization can be compromised. It requires technical knowledge, creativity and the ability to combine different techniques to achieve a specific objective.

Today, AI can assist with several stages of this process: from understanding a target environment and reviewing code to generating scripts, developing test cases and adapting existing techniques. Tasks that previously required hours of research and implementation may now be approached through a conversational interface and a series of iterations.

This does not eliminate the need for expertise. Understanding the target, identifying the right attack path, validating results and avoiding unintended consequences still require technical judgment.

However, **the barrier to entry is changing**.

A person who previously lacked the knowledge to implement a particular technique may now be able to experiment with it, understand its limitations and gradually refine the result with AI assistance. 

For a Red Team, this represents an opportunity to increase the speed and breadth of security assessments.

For a **malicious actor**, the same capabilities can lower the cost of experimentation and potentially increase the number of attack attempts.

The problem is not simply that attacks are becoming more sophisticated. It is that **creating and adapting them may be becoming easier and faster**.

**Blue Team.**

What does this mean for the people responsible for defending an organization?

For a Blue Team, the consequences can be frustrating.

Security analysts already operate in an environment characterized by alert fatigue, incomplete visibility, false positives and the constant need to prioritize incidents. Now, they must also consider a threat landscape in which technical experimentation can be accelerated by AI.

An attacker does not necessarily need to develop every component from scratch. They can use AI to understand existing techniques, modify scripts, generate variations, or troubleshoot implementation problems.

The result is an additional source of uncertainty: **how do you defend against attacks when the effort required to create them continues to decrease?**

There is also a psychological aspect to consider.

Blue Team members may feel that they are constantly chasing new techniques, trying to keep pace with tools that evolve faster than established detection and response processes. The concern is understandable, especially when the same AI capabilities that accelerate offensive experimentation are not yet fully integrated into the defensive workflow.

On the other hand, blue teams have the possibility to leverage new AI-based SOCs to intercept and mitigate this kind of attacks.

The question is whether organizations can integrate these capabilities effectively.

**The State of the Art.**

Vibe hacking is not a standardized technical discipline and AI-assisted attacks are not an entirely new category of threat. Rather, the term describes a broader evolution in how existing offensive capabilities can be developed and applied.

An AI system connected to internal resources may be exposed to prompt injection, unauthorized data access, insecure tool permissions, or vulnerabilities in the application surrounding the model. At the same time, AI-generated code can introduce conventional security flaws, including missing authorization checks, insecure configurations and inadequate input validation.

These are precisely the kinds of problems that require a combination of traditional security testing and AI-specific evaluation.

Established approaches such as penetration testing and Red Team exercises remain relevant, but they need to account for the behavior of AI models, the permissions granted to agents and the interactions between models and external systems.

**NIST** provides a foundation for understanding Red Teaming as a structured approach to adversarial evaluation, while the **OWASP GenAI Security Project** addresses risks specific to generative AI systems and provides guidance for testing their security.

The challenge is to integrate them into a continuous process in which AI-generated capabilities are assessed, security controls are validated and findings are translated into measurable improvements.

Vibe hacking highlights a fundamental shift in Cyber Security: the relationship between technical knowledge and technical execution is changing.

Red Teams can use AI to accelerate security assessments and explore a broader range of attack scenarios. Malicious actors can use the same capabilities to reduce the effort required to develop and adapt attacks. Blue Teams, meanwhile, must respond to an environment in which experimentation is becoming more accessible and the volume of potential threats may increase.

But this is not simply a race between AI-powered attackers and AI-powered defenders.

AI can make the creation of attacks easier. It can also make security testing and defensive analysis more accessible.

**The real challenge is ensuring that the speed of AI-assisted development is matched by the ability to detect, investigate and prevent what it makes possible.**
