---
title: Hacking as a Job: The Business Behind Cybercrime
date: 2026-08-31
short: Hacking is no longer only about technical skills: exploring the cybercrime ecosystem, ransomware, and why opportunity matters more than company size.
---

**"When I grow up, I want to be a hacker!"**

Nowadays, being a hacker can be a **job**, so we should expect younger generations to develop this kind of interest, if they have not already done so.

"Hacking" in the sense of being curious, of course, but not only that. Hacking in the more extreme sense of the term: the willingness to cause damage with the purpose of **making money**. In this context, it can effectively become a job.

Cybercrime has developed into a real **ecosystem**. There are people who develop tools, people who sell **compromised access**, people who rent out infrastructure, people who carry out attacks, and people who launder the money.
An attacker may also work **on commission**. An example could be: "Steal the Facebook account of someone I dislike." It does not take much effort to find people offering this kind of service on the **dark web**, sometimes even openly.

There is also **group-based hacking**, which can lead to larger and, consequently, more profitable incidents. One of the most direct ways to make money is **ransomware**: malicious software that, once established within a system, may move laterally to other, more sensitive assets inside an organization, such as a **Domain Controller**. It can then proceed with the large-scale encryption of data stored across the affected assets in order to demand a ransom.
And we are not talking about a few euros. The amounts involved can be substantial enough to buy **entire houses, properties, and land**.

Ransomware is one of those events that is often underestimated because organizations believe that **"it will never happen to us", "we are too small" or "we are not important enough to be of interest."**
However, an attacker does not always choose the victim. Often, the attacker chooses the **opportunity**. An exposed system, a reused password, an unprotected access point or an ineffective backup can turn even a **small company into a convenient target**.

Money has the same value regardless of whether it comes from a small company or a large organization. Refusing to pay can lead to further problems, particularly regarding **privacy**. Very often, ransomware operators do not simply demand payment: they may also threaten to **publish stolen data**, after having copied it remotely before encrypting the original systems.
At worst, the consequences may become serious enough to **threaten the survival of the business itself**.

And this is only the beginning of the potential profit. Beyond the ransom, attackers may have obtained data that can be sold on the **dark web** to other threat actors, who can in turn use it to make money through **phishing emails** and other forms of fraud.

In the past, carrying out an attack required very specific technical skills. Today, some of the **tools, compromised access, and even services** needed to conduct attacks can be purchased. This does not mean that anyone can become an expert hacker, but it does mean that **causing damage no longer necessarily requires being one**.

At the same time, being a hacker does not necessarily mean being a criminal. Working on the security of a system by **testing its vulnerabilities** and attempting to exploit them in order to understand how it can be compromised is one of the objectives of **ethical hackers and penetration testers**. These are completely legitimate roles, in which the client itself requests this type of security assessment.

Prevention and detection of these events are more complex than they may appear. The development of a ransomware incident often leaves observable signs behind: significant activity involving **large volumes of files** being created or modified, processes used for **data encryption**, the deletion of **shadow copies or snapshots**, and the use of **unusual file extensions**.

These indicators can help identify an ongoing attack, but they also highlight a broader issue: **cybersecurity is not only about preventing an attacker from entering**. It is also about recognizing what is happening once something has already gone wrong and being able to **respond before the damage becomes irreversible**.
